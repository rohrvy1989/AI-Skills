# Worked example (annotated)

A full sample issue written in the voice, on a deliberately non-finance topic to show the method travels anywhere. Annotations in `[brackets]` point out the move being made — they are not part of the piece.

---

**Subject line:** The slow death your phone hides from you

`[Curiosity gap. "Slow death" + "hides from you" promises a twist without revealing it. Not "Why phone batteries degrade."]`

*In today's issue, we explain why your phone's battery quietly gets worse every year — and why the fix isn't the one you've been told.*

`[One-line italic promise. The reader knows exactly what they'll understand by the end.]`

## The Story

A few months ago, my phone died at 38%.

`[Human opening — a tiny, concrete, slightly absurd personal moment. No "batteries are a complex electrochemical system" throat-clearing.]`

Not 2%. Not 5%. Thirty-eight. One minute it was fine, the next the screen went black in the middle of a text. I assumed the phone was broken. It wasn't. The battery was just old — and old batteries lie about how much they have left.

`[Widens from the scene to the real subject. The last line is a small hook that plants the central mystery.]`

Here's the thing. Every rechargeable battery is dying from the day you buy it. Not because the makers cut corners, but because of what a battery physically *is*.

`[Conversational connective ("Here's the thing"), then reframes the problem as inevitable, which raises the obvious question — answered next.]`

A lithium-ion battery — the kind in basically every phone — works by shuffling tiny charged particles back and forth between two sides every time you charge and drain it. Think of it like a crowd moving between two rooms through a doorway, over and over. Do that enough times, and the doorway gets scuffed, the rooms get cluttered, and fewer people make the trip. That "trip" is your charge. Fewer trips means less battery.

`[Jargon ("lithium-ion") defined in plain words on contact. Then one clean, globally legible analogy — a crowd through a doorway — for an idea most people never actually understood.]`

Now I know what you're probably thinking: so why not just build a bigger battery that lasts for years?

`[The engine. Surfaces the reader's exact next question, in their voice.]`

Well, makers could. But a bigger battery means a thicker, heavier phone — and every survey for two decades says people pick thin and light over long-lasting almost every time. So phones are designed for the showroom, not for year three.

`[Answers the question, and sneaks in a second insight — the incentive behind the design.]`

But the battery wearing out isn't the most interesting part. It's what your phone does about it.

`[One-line-style turn that redirects from the obvious story to the surprising one — classic structure.]`

As the battery ages, your phone quietly starts protecting itself. It slows the processor down so it never asks the tired battery for more power than it can safely give. That's why an old phone feels sluggish *and* dies early — same root cause. The slowdown isn't a glitch. It's a feature you were never told about.

`[Payoff-building reveal. Connects two things the reader experienced separately — slowness and short battery life — into one cause.]`

So what actually helps?

`[Another reader question, teeing up the practical part.]`

Mostly, it's heat and extremes. Charging to 100% and draining to 0% every day is the doorway-scuffing at its worst. Leaving the phone baking in a hot car is worse still. Keeping it loosely topped up, somewhere in the middle, and cool — that's the boring, unglamorous secret. No app required.

`[Practical answer, kept plain. Resists the urge to list ten tips — gives the one principle that matters.]`

And there you have it. The reason your phone feels old isn't that you've outgrown it or that some company flipped a switch to make you buy a new one. It's a doorway, scuffed by a few hundred ordinary days, doing exactly what physics says it must.

`[Reframe ending: flips the reader's instinctive story ("they're sabotaging me to sell phones") to the truer, quieter one. States the single keep-this idea.]`

So yeah, mine still dies at 38%. But at least now I know it's not lying to spite me — it's just tired.

`[Warm, self-aware sign-off that loops back to the opening scene.]`

Until then…

## Why this works (quick recap of the moves)

- **Opened human** (phone dying at 38%), never on "batteries are electrochemical cells."
- **Ran on questions** the reader actually has — why not a bigger battery? what helps? — answered in order.
- **Defined the one piece of jargon** (lithium-ion) instantly, then dropped it.
- **One analogy, globally legible** (a crowd through a doorway), reused as a through-line.
- **Marked the turns** with short redirect sentences ("But the battery wearing out isn't the most interesting part").
- **Reframed at the end** instead of recapping, and tied the close back to the opening.
- **No listicle, no hedging, no inflated words.** Reads like table talk.

> This example uses evergreen science, so it needs no citations. For a topical piece — a deal, a number, a recent event — you'd research and verify each fact first, then weave in light attribution with an inline link on the load-bearing claims ("NIST [finalised the standards](url)," "Reuters [reported](url)"). The voice stays the same; it just rests on checked, linked facts.
